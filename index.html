01 · The basic idea

What is AI?

Artificial intelligence is a broad name for computer systems that perform tasks we associate with human intelligence, such as recognising images, predicting outcomes or producing language. Most AI does not “think” like a person. It uses rules or patterns learned from examples to turn an input into an output.

A spam filter, for example, learns features that often appear in unwanted email. A generative chatbot learns statistical relationships in large collections of text, then predicts a useful sequence of words in response to a prompt. The result can sound original, even though the system is calculating likely patterns.

Verified fact: The OECD describes AI systems as using inputs and operational logic to produce predictions, recommendations, content or decisions. Read the OECD explanation (opens external website).

02 · A balanced view

A useful tool, with real trade-offs

AI’s effect depends on its data, design, purpose and the people making decisions around it. The same ability to generate convincing content can support learning—or make fraud easier.

＋

Where it can help

  • Study: explain a difficult idea in several ways, create practice questions or suggest a first outline.
  • Work: summarise routine information, translate a draft or spot patterns in large datasets.
  • Society: assist doctors with medical images and help scientists compare more possibilities.

My view is that AI is most useful as a fast assistant. It can widen access and save time, while a person still supplies context and judgement.

!

Where it can harm

  • Errors: generated answers may invent facts or sources while sounding certain.
  • Bias and privacy: unrepresentative or sensitive data can create unfair results or expose personal information.
  • Manipulation: fake voices, images and videos can make impersonation and fraud more believable.

Real case · Hong Kong, 2024 Scammers used deepfake versions of a company’s chief financial officer and colleagues in a video meeting. An employee transferred HK$200 million after believing the people on screen were real. The practical lesson: verify unusual requests through a separate, trusted channel.

Before relying on an AI output

Pause. Check. Decide.

  1. 01Check important claims against a trustworthy source.
  2. 02Remove private or confidential information.
  3. 03Ask who could be harmed if the answer is wrong.

03 · Looking ahead

What might happen next?

No one knows exactly how AI will develop. These are three realistic possibilities—not certain predictions.

01

AI may become more useful

AI could become a better tutor, creative assistant and research tool. It may help people complete difficult or time-consuming tasks more quickly. People will still need to check the results and make the final decisions.

02

We will need backup plans

Schools, hospitals and businesses may depend more on AI services. If one of those services stops working or is attacked, important work could be interrupted. People must still know how to work without AI.

03

AI will need more electricity

Real case · United States, 2026 PJM, an electricity-grid operator serving 67 million people, made a plan for the growing number of large data centres. The plan aims to add power safely and reduce data-centre demand during emergencies if necessary.

This does not mean AI will automatically cause blackouts. It means energy companies and governments need to prepare for higher demand.

My interpretation

The best approach is to use AI where it genuinely helps. People should remain responsible, protect private information and test AI carefully before using it for important decisions.

05 · Process note

How this website was made

I organised the page as a short journey: a definition, a simple process diagram, a comparison of benefits and risks, and three future questions. I used an editorial layout with large serif headings, generous white space and offset cards. The colours follow a sunset, moving from pale cream and yellow to amber, burnt orange and deep brown.

The site uses semantic HTML for its structure and CSS for the layout, colours and responsive design. I avoided external fonts, image libraries and complicated JavaScript, so the page loads quickly and has fewer points of failure. I tested keyboard focus, link visibility and the layout at narrow mobile widths. The diagram is made from HTML and CSS, so it stays sharp and readable on different screens.

I used OpenAI Codex to brainstorm the structure, draft and review code, and suggest revisions.
